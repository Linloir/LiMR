# First Rhapsody on the Red Cliffs

Su Shi · Song dynasty · 1082

> An original English rendering prepared for this LiMR example from the public-domain Chinese text, not a quotation from a published translation. Compare the [Chinese original](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/%E5%89%8D%E8%B5%A4%E5%A3%81%E8%B3%A6). Section headings and the reading prompts at the end are editorial additions.

## A boat beneath the Red Cliffs

In the autumn of the renxu year, on the day after the full moon of the seventh month, Master Su and his guests took a boat beneath the Red Cliffs. A gentle breeze came; the water lay unruffled. Raising his wine, he drank to his guests and recited the poem of the bright moon, singing the verse of the graceful lady. Before long the moon rose above the eastern hills and lingered among the stars of the Dipper and the Ox. A white haze lay across the river; the gleam of water met the sky. We let our reedlike boat go where it would, crossing that vast expanse. Boundless, we seemed to ride the wind through empty space, with no knowledge of where we might come to rest; buoyant, we seemed to have left the world behind, taking wing to join the immortals.

## A song against the gunwale

Our pleasure grew as we drank, and we tapped the side of the boat and sang:

> Oars of cassia, paddles of fragrant wood,
> striking the moonlit emptiness, pressing upstream through the light.
> Far away my longing travels;
> I gaze toward the beloved at the edge of the sky.

One guest played a bamboo flute, following the song. Its low notes sounded like grievance and longing, like weeping and an appeal. Their lingering threads seemed never to break. They could have stirred a hidden dragon to dance in a deep ravine, or made a widow in a solitary boat weep.

## The guest's sorrow

Master Su grew solemn. Straightening his robe, he sat upright and asked, “Why does it sound so?”

The guest replied, “‘The moon is bright, the stars are few; the crows and magpies fly south.’ Are these not the words of Cao Mengde? To the west lies Xiakou, to the east Wuchang. Mountains and rivers wind around one another, densely green. Was this not the place where Mengde was brought to defeat by young Zhou? When he had taken Jingzhou and descended upon Jiangling, he followed the river east. His warships stretched a thousand li; his banners hid the sky. Pouring wine beside the river and laying his spear across his knees, he composed his verse. He was indeed a hero of his age. And where is he now?

“You and I fish and gather firewood on these riverbanks, keeping company with fish and shrimp, befriending deer. We steer a boat no bigger than a leaf and raise our gourd cups to one another. Our lives are mayflies lodged between heaven and earth; we are a single grain in the immense sea. I grieve that our lives last only an instant, and envy the great river its endless course. I would roam with flying immortals, embrace the bright moon, and endure forever. Knowing this cannot suddenly be attained, I entrust the lingering sound to the sorrowful wind.”

## Water and moonlight

Master Su said, “Do you also understand the water and the moon? Water passes like this, yet it has never gone away. The moon waxes and wanes like that, yet in the end it neither grows nor diminishes. Viewed from the side of change, heaven and earth cannot remain the same even for an instant. Viewed from the side of what does not change, all things and we ourselves are without end. What, then, is there to envy?

“Besides, everything between heaven and earth has its owner. If it is not ours, we should not take even the smallest part. Only the clear breeze upon the river and the bright moon among the hills become sound when our ears receive them, and color when our eyes encounter them. No one forbids us to take them; no use can exhaust them. They are creation's inexhaustible store, for you and me to enjoy together.”

## Dawn in the east

The guest smiled with delight. We rinsed our cups and poured again. When the food and fruit were finished, the cups and dishes lay scattered about. We reclined together in the boat, resting against one another, unaware that the east had already grown light.

## Space after reading

The following prompts were written for LiMR and are not part of Su Shi's original work.

The piece moves through both time and emotion. Mark “Our pleasure grew,” “grew solemn,” and “smiled with delight,” then return to their paragraphs and ask what brings each change about. The conversation stays with the same river and moon, but changes the angle from which they are seen.

One image can hold both sensory experience and abstract thought. “The gleam of water met the sky” first offers a scene; “Water passes like this” turns the river into a way of thinking about time. Add separate notes to these passages and try to explain the connection.

You can also select “Viewed from the side of change” and “Viewed from the side of what does not change.” Do the two perspectives contradict each other, or address different kinds of doubt? Keep the question open before settling on an interpretation.

Finally, reread the opening and the ending. The narrative begins with the rising moon and ends as the east grows light. It does not tell us that every question has been resolved; it leaves us with shared wine and the companions resting in the boat. What remains unsaid can become a margin note of your own.
